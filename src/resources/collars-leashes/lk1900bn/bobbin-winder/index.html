Winding LK-1900 bobbins with a standalone winder

When I first started using my Juki LK-1900 bartacker to sew collars & leashes, I considered using a standalone (aftermarket) bobbin winder. I thought that would be easier than the machine's built-in winder. [1] The LK-1900 uses style A, class 15 bobbins. This is the only winder I found that works with those (and the high-capacity bobbin[2] too):

As the photo notes, it has some shortcomings. I don't recommend having one (I almost never use mine). But, if you wanted to wind bobbins separately, it's the only one I could find. This page will save someone time if they need to do it. It's just a generic, no-name product sold by many online. The one I bought was ASIN: B07RJL9XHY (seller CKPSMS). I chose that seller because the product photos were more informative than other sellers; printed instructions were included (the english is a little hard to understand, but better than nothing).

Shortcomings

It works. But:

  1. Large (industrial) spools of thread won't fit the stand.

  2. The shutoff sensor is light-based. If there's too much ambient light, it won't shutoff. (The sensor is mounted on a shiny silver steel plate. Reflected light may make it worse.).

  3. The sensors are mounted a little low, not looking through the middle of the bobbin.

  4. The tension disk is problematic in a few ways:

    1. It's too close to the bobbin. You'll adjust the vertical height of the tension disk so the thread winds evenly up/down the bobbin. But, being so close, it won't let the thread travel as far (up/down) as it needs to. You end up with a bulge (donut) in the center of the bobbin. You can sew with that. But, you're not using the entire bobbin's capacity. (Increasing disk tension usually helps bobbins wind flatter. But, I think this is too close for that to work enough.).

    2. The center silver knob adjusts the height of the tension disk. But, it's not long enough to raise the disk high enough to center the bulge within the bobbin.

    3. Because of the way the the thread stand was fit on a small baseplate, the tension-disk's guide hole points in a direction that prevents routing the thread completely around the disk, out the guide hole (the way you do it with the LK-1900's bobbin-winder). You enter through the guide hole, but only wrap 3/4 around (clockwise. Then, go straight to the bobbin from there.). This may not be a problem. It just seems incorrect.

Regarding #3 (sensors mounted low): This is more of an issue due to the way the winding bulges (#4a). The sensors look through the area near the bottom flange. The bulge could grow larger than the bobbin's diameter (before that area near the flange fills up enough to stop the winder). If it wound evenly, the sensor's aim wouldn't matter much. It's not a problem if the bulge winds too tall (won't fit in the bobbin case). You can manually unwind some, and wind that into the unfilled area next to the flanges. It takes a minute.

Easy improvements

Most of those shortcomings can be improved without much effort:

  1. Replace the silver center (height-adjusting) knob with a bolt that's longer (so the disk height can be adjusted higher). The knob's thread is M4 x 0.7. I replaced mine with a 35mm-long hex-head bolt. But, note:

    1. A longer bolt will enable you to adjust the tension assembly too high. There is a small post underneath the tension disk. It slips into the plastic box. That post will slip out when you elevate the tension disks too high.

    2. There is a spring under the tension assembly. You'll notice that stop making (enough) contact before the post slips out of the hole. The disk assembly will be loose, wobbly. You could add a washer under the spring to improve that. But, you can't keep going much higher before the previously mentioned post slips out.

    3. Important: there is a circuit board underneath. From the point my bolt starts threading, it can screw down 1/2" before it touches the circuit board.

  2. Raise the sensors so they're looking through the bobbin's middle. Use a washer underneath the plate they're mounted on. You may need a longer screw. It's M4 x 0.7. A 10mm length works fine. (FYI: there's nothing underneath this screw inside the box. No risk of it being too long and going too far down.)

  3. Use a stand-alone thread stand. I have one with a cast-iron base (Amazon ASIN: B01ELYA1HC. That same stand is also sold with a plastic base. The weight of cast-iron is worth the extra cost. I cut a piece of felt to fit the bottom of the stand so it wouldn't scratch tabletops, glued it on.).

  4. Tape some poster-board around the end of the winder where the sensors are (create some shade). It might help to paint the shiny plate black, or affix some dark-blue painter's tape so it's not as reflective.

Ultimate improvement

The winder works pretty well with the above simple fixes. But, you'll still have bulging (uneven) winds because the tension-disk & bobbin are too close. If you don't use it often, that wouldn't matter much. I was intrigued about how to fix that too. I mounted the winder on a new (larger) platform so I could move the tension disk further:

That extends the tension disk 11" from the bobbin (the same as the LK-1900's distance). Since I use the high-capacity bobbin (32.6% wider, the thread travels up/down that much further), I extended the tension disk that much further (3-5/8") thinking it might wind better. I also mounted the thread-stand parts so they work with large cones. That's oriented so the thread can enter & exit the guide hole the way you do it with the LK-1900.

Some notes:

  • I glue the t-nuts in. If you assemble/reassemble this setup a few times, they could get loose, push (fall) out the backside. This is especially true if you sink one in (using a bolt, washer & nut) to test the depth of the hole (forstner bit) you drilled, and remove it the same way (from the opposite side). Installing & removing will cause it to grip less strongly when you finally install it. I used a Q-tip to smear wood glue around the countersunk hole. That helps it resist getting loose over time, falling out.

  • I sand everythign smooth - especially rounding the corners to be soft to the touch. I apply one coat of polyeurathane (satin) just to seal the wood against grime. I apply a heavy coat, working it in, letting it soak in a lot. This can raise the wood fibers, creating a rough surface when it dries. After it dries, I lightly wipe with fine steel wool to knock down the raised fibers.

  • Using the thread stand that comes with the winder, I quickly encountered a new "shortcoming:" the thread comes out of the stand's suspended loop (above the cone). I attached some electrical tape to make a hanging "flap" that blocks that path.

  • That thread-stand's loop has to be 2-3" higher for industrial cones. I made a "Z-stand." It's 3" tall. If you make that, it has to fit between the winder box & extension arm (when stored. My space is 3-1/8". Technically (the pdf drawing), that space should be 3-1/4". But, I have the box & arm recessed inside the edge of the board a little.). Note: I made that stand so it would guide the thread out from underneath it (following the / of the Z). If it was positioned the other way, the thread would snag under the top of the Z.)

  • If your thread's cone has a large inside diameter, you can slide a "fender washer" (3/16" inside diameter) over the stand's center post. That will keep the cone more centered. (The washer will be elevated a little by the post's nut.). The thread I use has an inside diameter over 1". I can slide a 3/4" or 1" washer over the post. Then the cone stays more automatically centered.

Things I might do differently:

  1. Raising the thread-arm (Z-stand): the thread-arm has to be 2-3" taller (I did 3"). It may be easier to do using a t-nut, threaded rod, coupler & nuts (as jam nuts, securing all the pieces together. Maybe a couple wingnuts instead of nuts for easy, tool-free disassembly/assembly.). But, they have to be M4 x 0.7 thread. Where I live, metric isn't common. I found all those parts except the coupler at local stores. It wasn't worth it to order the coupler online. Making the Z-stand from 1/2" x 1/8"-thick aluminum bar-stock is less expensive than buying those parts. But, it's more work. If you don't like that kind of work, then you can buy those metric parts online.

  2. Tension-disk height adjusting bolt: I used an #8-32 threaded insert (and 2-1/4" threaded rod with two nuts jammed together at one end creating a "knob" to turn with my fingers). Howwever, I discovered that not all #8 machine threads pass through the barrel of that tension-disk assembly. The barrel was made for M4 x 0.7. Apparently that dimension is within the tolerance for #8 machine threads. Some threaded rods & bolts fit, some didn't. If I did this again, I would use #6-32 (threaded insert & machine screw or threaded rod). That would be strong enough. If you live somewhere metric is plentiful, you could use M4 x 0.7 threaded insert & a long bolt.

  3. You can replace the tension disk with the same one used on the LK-1900 (#141-13468). It's not very expensive, but better quality. It uses an 11/64"-40 thread. You should have that tap/die because it's a common thread size used by other things on the LK-1900. I wrote about that in the LK-1900's page regarding making custom arch face-plate screws. 11/64-40 is a little larger diameter than #8. I believe you could tap a 8-32 threaded insert into this size.

Notes

[1] The main reason I considered using a standalone bobbin winder is because it's hard to get the bobbin to start winding on the LK-1900's winder (enough grip to start). That was 50/50 for me. But, I learned to drill a hole in the side of the bobbin to get enough friction to start windining. That works 100%.

Another reason I thought I wanted an external winder: the machine's winder doesn't operate independently. If you need to wind a bobbin, the whole machine operates. That seems like a lot of wear & tear just to wind a bobbin. (Normally you'll wind as you sew. That's not a problem. I almost never have to use the control-panel feature to wind one immediately. As often as I've had to do it, I could've wound the bobbin by hand. It takes 3-4 minutes.). Note: If you do wind a bobbin using the LK-1900's control-panel feature, be sure to unthread the machine back past the takeup lever. Otherwise, the thread (whipping up/down with the takeup lever) can be pulled inside the takeup lever's vertical slot, jamming the machine.

So, I didn't really need an external winder. I only put this page together in case anyone does. This winder is the only one I found that works with this size bobbin. This page will save someone time if they need one.

[2] If you upgrade your LK-1900 to use high-capacity bobbins, the standalone winder works with those too. But:

  1. The tension-disk height needs to be a little higher. A 40mm-long screw is more appropriate (but the 35mm works). Remember: there is a circuit board underneath. If you screw this down 1/2" you'll be close to touching the board. So, this screw should be chosen to fit. Or, stack washers under the bolt's head to reduce its effective length.

  2. The sensor plate needs to be even higher. You'll need a 12- or 15mm-long screw (another washer underneath). There's nothing underneath this screw (inside the box) for it to interfere with. It can be any length.

  3. The half-circle cutout in the sensor plate needs to be enlarged with a dremel to accommodate the larger bobbin's diameter.

  4. To the extent moving the tension disks further away helps normal bobbins wind more evenly (flatter), it may help to go even further. The high-capacity bobbins are 32.6% wider (the thread travels up/down that much further). It could benefit if the disks are moved 32.6% further (i.e., an additional 3-5/8" to 14-5/8" apart).
